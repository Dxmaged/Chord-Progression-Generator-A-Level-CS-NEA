How to Use the Chord Progression Generator

Follow these steps to generate a chord progression:

  1. Select a Tonic (the key of your progression).
  2. Choose a Genre (Pop, Jazz, Rock, etc.).
  3. Enter the Number of Chords you want (between 3 and 8).
  4. Choose an Instrument to display the generated chords.
  5. Click the Generate Progression button.
  6. Your chord progression will appear below!

Understanding the Parameters

Below is an explanation of each input parameter and the choices available.

Tonic (Key)

The tonic is the root note of your chord progression. It determines the overall key of your progression.

  • C: A bright and neutral key, commonly used in pop, classical, and electronic music.
  • G: A common key in rock and folk, known for its open and natural sound.
  • D: Frequently used in acoustic and folk music due to its bright tone.
  • A: Used in blues, rock, and country music, giving a warm feel.
  • E: A powerful key often used in rock, metal, and blues.
  • F: Has a rich and warm character, often used in jazz and classical.
  • B♭: Common in jazz and brass instruments, giving a mellow and smooth feel.

Genre

The genre determines the type of chord progressions used. Each genre has a unique musical feel.

  • Pop: Pop music is characterized by catchy melodies, simple harmonies, and repetitive structures. Common progressions include I - V - vi - IV.
  • Jazz: Jazz features complex harmonies and improvisation. It often uses progressions like ii - V - I.
  • Blues: Blues is built on expressive phrasing and dominant chords. It commonly follows I - IV - V.
  • Rock: Rock music is energetic and riff-driven, often using progressions like I - IV - V.
  • Ballad: Ballads are slow, emotional songs using rich harmonies such as i - VI - III - VII.
  • Flamenco: A Spanish folk style known for its dramatic minor progressions like i - ♭II - ♭III.
  • Middle Eastern: This genre uses microtonal scales and exotic harmonies, often featuring i - ♭II - ♭VII.
  • Dreamy: Dreamy music is atmospheric and ethereal, using progressions like I - II - V - I.
  • Experimental: Experimental music pushes harmonic boundaries, often using dissonance and unexpected chord shifts like i° - ♭II - iv.

Number of Chords

The number of chords determines how many chords appear in the generated progression.

  • 3 chords: Simple and repetitive, commonly used in blues and folk.
  • 4 chords: The most common format in pop and rock songs.
  • 5-6 chords: Provides more variation, common in jazz and film music.
  • 7-8 chords: Longer progressions, often used in complex or cinematic music.
